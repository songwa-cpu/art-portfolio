Cowrie: The Shells That Echo the Past

January 8, 2025 · 3 min read

After moving to Ghana in June 2023, I was introduced to the history of Cowrie shells and hadn't anticipated falling down a rabbit hole of research. Although I've seen these shells in modern jewelry, art, and even gift shops, I had simply seen them as an "aesthetic."

Cowrie shells were once more than just a fashion statement. They were used as a currency in many pre-colonial regions such as West and East Africa, India, China, and the Pacific Islands. These shells are derived from sea snails, specifically the Monetaria Moneta, a species native to the tropical waters of the Indian Ocean. Cowries were considered a reliable and durable currency that could withstand harsh conditions throughout its exports and day-to-day use in trades.

Cowries are almost impossible to counterfeit and were once considered to have a limited supply from one of the greatest exporters, the Maldives. There was an increasing demand for Cowries especially between the 16th and 18th centuries. West African regions, such as modern-day Ghana and Nigeria, were experiencing an expansion of the transatlantic slave trade and Cowries were the preferred form of transaction.

Victims of the slave trade in the mainland would be initially traded for a couple hundred Cowries. They would often be sold multiple times before reaching the coast and inhumanely transacted with more Cowries to European settlers. At the peak of the trade in the 17th and 18th centuries, victims could be quoted to be worth over 100,000 Cowries.

After over 400 years of the slave trade, it is approximated that at least 6–7 million West Africans were captured and sold. Imagine the millions of Cowries that had to be exported to West Africa alone to fulfil the demand of the trade. Besides their unfortunate use in trading for human life, they were also used to pay labourers and the purchasing of goods. The Cowrie shell became deeply embedded within many African regions' economies as a standardized currency.

Naturally, this small white shell became a great symbol of power, status and wealth. Despite its regrettably sad use for the slave trade, many African countries preserved this beautiful shell and incorporated it into their ceremonial pieces, divination practices, jewelry and clothing. The Cowrie innately became a symbol of resistance and an integral part of Africa's cultural identity.

Although perceived as a small and insignificant shell to the unknowing, its strength and durability are a traditional motif connecting the past with present and future generations. Much like the Cowrie, the millions of Africans who were traded, were inexplicably resilient and withstood the harsh and violent conditions they had to endure.

Today, the Cowries' legacy still withstands and is seen in contemporary art, jewelry, decoration and fashion. Ghana in particular calls its modern paper currency the "Cedi," which is an Akan term for Cowrie. Acknowledging the historical significance of the original Cedi, Ghana symbolically continues economic pre-colonial trading practices. Its modern use and wear further emphasizes the preservation of cultural heritage and identity across Africa and the Diaspora.
